A row update starts with a lookup in an index. Its effects then reach the undo log, the redo log, and eventually the data file. These four simulations follow that path, including what a concurrent reader sees and what a crash can leave behind.

## Split a clustered B+ tree

The leaves of an InnoDB clustered index contain the rows, ordered by the clustered key. This model gives each leaf room for just three keys. Insert **40** into the starting leaf to split it and see a separator copied into a new root.

## Follow a row update

Try **BEGIN**, **INSERT**, and **UPDATE name**. Watch the row change in the buffer pool while an undo record saves the old value and redo records accumulate. **COMMIT** flushes redo in this model. The data page can stay dirty until a later flush. [InnoDB redo log](https://dev.mysql.com/doc/dev/mysql-server/8.4.11/PAGE_INNODB_REDO_LOG.html)

<InnoDbFigure kind="update" />

## Read an older version with MVCC

A reader opens a snapshot while another transaction changes a balance from **$100** to **$50**. Step through the four events to compare how InnoDB can walk back through undo and how PostgreSQL can examine retained tuple versions.

<InnoDbFigure kind="mvcc" />

## Recover a torn page with doublewrite

A crash during a data-file write can leave a page partly old and partly new. With doublewrite recovery enabled, InnoDB can restore a complete copy before applying redo. Press **Crash now**, then **Step** to follow the recovery. [MySQL doublewrite buffer documentation](https://dev.mysql.com/doc/refman/8.4/en/innodb-doublewrite-buffer.html)
